Reading the Coverage View Layout

▶

The Coverage View is the central diagnostic display for understanding how well your course content maps to academic standards. Rather than requiring you to manually cross-reference assignments, quizzes, and activities against a list of standards, the Coverage View consolidates everything into a structured, row-based layout that makes gaps and strengths immediately visible. To use this view effectively, you need to understand exactly what information appears in each row, how the summary headline works, and what each status indicator is telling you about the state of your course design.

Every row in the Coverage View corresponds to a single standard that has been mapped to your course. The row is not just a label — it is a compact dashboard for that standard, surfacing several distinct pieces of information side by side so you can assess the standard's coverage without navigating elsewhere.

The standard's code and full statement appear together in each row. The code (such as a Common Core identifier or a state-specific alphanumeric label) gives you a shorthand reference, while the full statement tells you precisely what the standard requires learners to know or do. Having both in the same row means you never have to look up what a code means — the meaning is right there, providing immediate context for every other data point in that row.

The Bloom's Taxonomy cognitive level assigned to the standard is also displayed in each row. Bloom's Taxonomy organizes cognitive demand into a hierarchy — from lower-order thinking skills like Remember and Understand, through mid-level skills like Apply and Analyze, to higher-order skills like Evaluate and Create. When the Coverage View shows a standard's Bloom's level, it is telling you the depth of thinking that the standard itself demands of learners. A standard coded at the Analyze level, for example, expects students to do more than recall facts — they must break down information and examine relationships. Seeing this level directly in the row helps you immediately spot which standards require the most cognitively demanding instructional and assessment strategies.

Aligned content counts for assignments, quiz items, and activities are displayed in separate columns within each row. These counts represent the number of individual content pieces — a single assignment, a single quiz question, a single activity — that have been explicitly connected to that standard. For instance, a row might show two assignments, five quiz items, and one activity aligned to a particular standard, giving you a concrete picture of how much instructional weight is being placed on it. A count of zero in any column is just as informative — it signals a potential gap in how that standard is being addressed.

A Status indicator summarizes the overall coverage state of the standard in a single, scannable label. Rather than requiring you to interpret the raw counts yourself, the status indicator applies a defined set of rules to those counts and presents a conclusion. The statuses form a tiered hierarchy, each one reflecting a meaningfully different level of instructional engagement with the standard.

The four tiered status indicators work as follows:

  • Not Aligned — This status means the standard has not been mapped to the course at all. It appears in contexts where you are viewing a standard that exists in the system but has no connection to your course structure. Nothing has been associated with it.
  • Not Addressed — This status means the standard has been mapped to the course — it is on your list of standards to cover — but no assignment, quiz item, or activity has yet been aligned to it. The standard is acknowledged but completely unaddressed in terms of content.
  • Practiced Only — This status means content has been aligned to the standard, but that content consists entirely of activities. No assignment or quiz item is connected to it. The standard is being practiced or introduced, but it has not been formally assessed.
  • Assessed — This status means at least one assignment or at least one quiz item is aligned to the standard. Formal assessment has occurred. This is the highest status in the tier and does not require both an assignment and a quiz item — either one is sufficient.

Understanding the distinction between assessment and practice is essential for interpreting these statuses correctly. In the Coverage View's logic, assignments and quiz items are both classified as assessment content. They represent formal, evaluative tasks — work that is graded or scored and that provides evidence of student mastery. A standard is considered assessed as soon as a single assignment or a single quiz item is aligned to it. You do not need both. If you have already aligned an assignment to a standard, that standard is assessed regardless of whether any quiz items are also aligned.

Activities, by contrast, are the only content type classified as practice. Activities are typically lower-stakes instructional tasks — interactive exercises, discussions, or preparatory work — that help students engage with a standard without constituting formal evaluation. When activities are the only content aligned to a standard, the standard has been practiced but not assessed, which is exactly what the Practiced Only status communicates. This distinction matters because a standard that is only practiced may not generate the evidence of learning needed for grading, reporting, or accountability purposes.

The Coverage Headline Summary sits above the individual rows and aggregates data across all standards mapped to the course. Rather than requiring you to scan every row to understand the big picture, the headline surfaces two critical course-wide metrics at a glance. First, it shows the proportion of standards that are assessed — for example, indicating that 18 out of 24 mapped standards have at least one assignment or quiz item aligned to them. Second, it highlights the count of standards with a cognitive-depth gap. A cognitive-depth gap exists when the Bloom's level of the standard's aligned learning outcomes is lower than the Bloom's level the standard itself requires. The headline flags how many standards are in this situation, drawing your attention to cases where the instructional or assessment design may not be reaching the cognitive depth the standard demands.

The Bloom's Level column is the foundation for that cognitive-depth analysis. Each row displays the Bloom's level assigned to the standard, and the system compares that level against the Bloom's levels found in the learning outcomes that are aligned to the standard's content. If a standard demands Evaluate-level thinking but all of its aligned outcomes only reach Remember or Understand, a gap is flagged. The Bloom's level column makes this comparison transparent — you can see at a glance which standards sit at higher cognitive tiers and therefore require the most careful alignment between the standard's demands and the design of assessments and activities.

The aligned content counts per standard — broken out into separate figures for assignments, quiz items, and activities — give you the granular data behind the status indicator. Consider the following example scenarios:

Standard Bloom's Level Assignments Quiz Items Activities Status
ELA.RL.9-10.1 Analyze 2 5 1 Assessed
ELA.RL.9-10.4 Understand 0 0 3 Practiced Only
ELA.RL.9-10.6 Evaluate 0 0 0 Not Addressed
ELA.W.9-10.2 Create 1 0 2 Assessed

In the table above, ELA.RL.9-10.1 is robustly assessed — two assignments and five quiz items are aligned, along with one activity. ELA.RL.9-10.4 has three activities but no formal assessment content, placing it in Practiced Only status. ELA.RL.9-10.6 has nothing aligned at all, making it Not Addressed — a clear curriculum gap. ELA.W.9-10.2 reaches Assessed status through a single assignment, even though no quiz items are aligned, illustrating that one piece of assessment content is sufficient.

Reading the Coverage View layout fluently means treating each row as a multi-dimensional profile of a single standard, interpreting the status indicators within their defined hierarchy, and using the headline summary to orient your attention toward the most pressing course-wide gaps before drilling into individual rows for detail.
