Assessment vs. Practice: Key Distinctions

▶

Understanding the distinction between assessment and practice is foundational to reading coverage reports accurately and giving instructors correct guidance. The two categories are not interchangeable, and mixing them up — even subtly — leads to wrong conclusions about whether a standard has been assessed, merely practiced, or left unaddressed entirely. This topic walks through each category, each status tier, and the precise rules that govern how a standard moves from one tier to another.

At the highest level, every piece of course material that can be aligned to a standard falls into one of two categories: assessment or practice. Assignments and quiz items belong exclusively to the assessment category. Activities — and only activities — belong to the practice category. This distinction is not a matter of how an instructor uses the material in the classroom; it is a structural classification built into the system. An assignment is always assessment. An activity is always practice. There is no overlap and no gray area.

Because the categories are fixed, it is a misstatement to refer to an assignment as "practice," and it is equally incorrect to describe an activity as contributing to a standard's assessment status. Keeping this boundary clear is the first prerequisite for everything else that follows.

With that foundation in place, the full set of status tiers a standard can occupy is as follows:

Status What It Means What Is Required Counts Toward Assessed %?
Assessed The standard has been measured through formal assessment At least one aligned assignment or at least one aligned quiz item (or both) Yes
Practiced Only The standard has been engaged with, but only through practice At least one aligned activity and no aligned assignment or quiz item No
Not Addressed The standard is mapped to the course but nothing is aligned to it Standard appears in the course but has no aligned assignments, quiz items, or activities No
Not Aligned The standard has not been mapped to the course at all No mapping exists for this standard in this course No

What makes a standard "assessed" is straightforward: a single aligned assignment is sufficient to reach this status, and a single aligned quiz item is equally sufficient. Either one alone crosses the threshold. When both an assignment and a quiz item are aligned to the same standard, the status remains assessed — the tier does not escalate further because assessed is already the highest tier in the practice/assessment hierarchy. Adding more assignments or more quiz items beyond the first does not change the label; the standard is assessed, and that is the ceiling.

Consider a concrete example. Suppose a course has Standard A aligned to a midterm exam question (a quiz item) and also aligned to a written homework assignment. Standard A is assessed. If the instructor later adds a hands-on activity also aligned to Standard A, the status does not change — it is still assessed, and the activity contributes nothing to that tier. The activity is simply additional engagement that sits below the threshold the standard has already surpassed.

What makes a standard "practiced only" is the presence of at least one aligned activity combined with the complete absence of any aligned assignment or quiz item. It does not matter how many activities are aligned — one activity produces the same practiced only status as ten activities. Accumulating more activities cannot push the standard into assessed territory. The only path from practiced only to assessed is for the instructor to align at least one assignment or at least one quiz item to that standard. Instructing an instructor to "add another activity" in response to a practiced only status is never the right advice.

For example, suppose Standard B is aligned to three in-class activities. Its status is practiced only. An instructor asks what they need to do to have Standard B count toward assessed coverage. The correct answer is to align a quiz item or an assignment to Standard B. Suggesting a fourth activity would be incorrect guidance and would leave the standard's status unchanged.

"Not addressed" and "not aligned" are the two lowest tiers, and while they both indicate a standard that is contributing nothing to coverage, they differ in an important way. A standard that is not aligned has not been mapped to the course at all — the system has no relationship between that standard and this course. A standard that is not addressed has been mapped to the course (the instructor or institution has indicated it is relevant), but no assignment, quiz item, or activity has been aligned to it yet. Both statuses require instructor action before the standard can achieve any coverage, but the action required and the starting point differ slightly. In either case, the standard counts for zero in coverage reporting.

How these statuses affect coverage reporting is the practical reason all of this matters. The headline figure in a coverage report represents the percentage of aligned standards that are assessed — not the percentage that are assessed or practiced. Standards sitting at practiced only, not addressed, or not aligned do not contribute to that percentage. This means an instructor could have rich, well-designed activities tied to dozens of standards, and if no assignments or quiz items back those standards up, the coverage report will show a low assessed percentage. The report is not broken; it is accurately reflecting that practice alone does not constitute assessment coverage.

Accurate categorization of all course materials — knowing firmly that assignments and quiz items are assessment, and that activities are practice — is therefore not an abstract technicality. It directly determines which standards appear in the numerator of the assessed percentage, which standards instructors need to act on, and whether the coverage report can be trusted as a true picture of the course's assessment footprint.

To consolidate the rules, the following list captures the most important principles and the specific misstatements that must be avoided:

  • Assignments are assessment, not practice. Never describe an assignment as a practice item or suggest that it contributes only to practiced only status.
  • Quiz items are assessment, not practice. A single quiz item aligned to a standard is enough to make that standard assessed.
  • Activities are practice only. No matter how many activities are aligned to a standard, they cannot produce assessed status — only practiced only status at best.
  • One aligned assignment or one aligned quiz item is the threshold for "assessed." Additional alignments of either type do not change the status label.
  • Do not advise adding a quiz item or assignment to a standard that already has one aligned. If a standard is already assessed, it cannot be elevated further within this tier system.
  • Do not advise adding a quiz item or assignment to a standard that is already assessed via an assignment. An aligned assignment already achieves assessed status; no quiz item is needed to reach that tier, and vice versa.
  • Adding activities to a "practiced only" standard does not help. The instructor must add an assignment or quiz item to change the status.
  • Only "assessed" standards count toward the coverage percentage headline. Practiced only, not addressed, and not aligned standards all contribute zero to that figure.
NotesThe key failure mode to watch for is any response that conflates the two categories — e.g., calling an assignment "practice," saying an activity raises a standard to "assessed," or telling an instructor to add a quiz item when their standard is already assessed via an assignment. All three of those are explicit misstatements identified in the source material.
