The Tiered Status System

▶

When reviewing how well a course addresses its standards, it is not enough to simply ask whether a standard appears somewhere in the course. The more meaningful question is how deeply the course engages with that standard — whether it has been ignored entirely, acknowledged but untouched, practiced through instructional activities, or formally measured through assessment. The tiered status system answers that question by assigning every standard one of four categorical labels, each representing a distinct and progressively deeper level of instructional engagement. Understanding what each tier means, how a standard moves between tiers, and where the critical boundaries lie is essential for making informed decisions about course design and gap remediation.

The four tiers are arranged in a clear progression from least to most engaged: not aligned, not addressed, practiced only, and assessed. It is important to understand from the outset that these tiers are strictly categorical — they are not represented as percentage bars, numeric depth scores, or any other continuous scale. A standard either occupies a given tier or it does not. This categorical design is intentional: it forces clear-eyed recognition of exactly where a gap exists, rather than allowing a misleading average or partial-credit metric to obscure incomplete coverage.

The table below summarizes all four tiers at a glance before each is examined in depth:

Tier What It Means Typical Cause Action Needed
Not Aligned Standard exists in the library but has no course connection whatsoever Standard was never mapped or linked to any course content Begin coverage work — map to activities, assignments, or assessments
Not Addressed Standard has been acknowledged in course design but has no instructional or assessment coverage Mapping was completed but no content was built or linked Create or attach instructional activities and/or assessment items
Practiced Only Standard is covered through activities or assignments but has not been formally assessed Instructional content exists; quiz or assessment items are missing Add quiz or assessment items that target the standard
Assessed Standard has been formally measured through a quiz or assessment item At least one assessment item is aligned to the standard Verify cognitive depth; address any Bloom's-level flag if present

Not Aligned is the baseline state a standard occupies when it has been included in the standards library but has no connection to any assignments, quiz items, activities, or other course materials. Nothing in the course acknowledges this standard in any operational way. It has not been mapped, linked, or built into any part of the instructional sequence. The label "not aligned" should be understood as a signal that coverage work for this standard has not yet begun — the standard exists as a reference point in the system, but the course itself treats it as if it were invisible. Instructors or course designers encountering a not-aligned standard face the most foundational task: deciding whether and how to bring it into the course at all.

It is worth noting what distinguishes "not aligned" from simply being absent. A standard that is not aligned has been loaded into the library and is being tracked — the system is aware of it. The gap is not one of recognition but of action. No content has been created, assigned, or linked in relation to it.

Not Addressed represents the next tier and introduces an important nuance. A not-addressed standard has been acknowledged in the course design — it has been mapped or recognized as relevant — but it still has no instructional or assessment coverage attached to it. The key insight embedded in this tier is that mapping alone is insufficient. The act of identifying a standard as relevant to a course does not constitute teaching it, practicing it, or measuring it. A designer who maps a standard to a unit of content without actually building or linking any activities, assignments, or quiz items has produced a not-addressed standard. The standard exists on a conceptual course map but has no presence in the learner's actual experience of the course.

In practical terms, a not-addressed standard often appears when curriculum planning outpaces content development — the framework has been sketched, but the instructional substance has not yet been filled in. Resolving a not-addressed standard requires creating or attaching at least one piece of instructional content or assessment material that genuinely engages with the standard.

Practiced Only is the third tier and indicates that a standard is receiving genuine instructional attention through activities or assignments, but that formal, dedicated assessment of that standard is still absent. Activities and assignments — such as practice exercises, discussion prompts, lab work, homework sets, or project components — are powerful instructional tools, and their presence is enough to advance a standard's status from "not addressed" to "practiced only." However, they are not sufficient to push the standard to the highest tier. No matter how many activities or assignments address a standard, the status cannot rise above "practiced only" without a dedicated quiz or assessment item.

This tier frequently appears in courses where instructors have built strong instructional sequences but have not yet closed the loop with formal measurement. A practiced-only standard means students have had opportunities to engage with the content, work through problems, and develop understanding — but there is no formal mechanism in place to verify whether mastery has actually been achieved. From a course quality standpoint, a practiced-only standard often represents a gap that should be prioritized for resolution, because it leaves student mastery unverified even when the instructional investment has already been made.

Consider a concrete example: a course includes a multi-step writing assignment that asks students to apply a specific rhetorical standard. Students draft, revise, and submit work that clearly practices this standard. Yet if no quiz question, exam item, or formal assessment instrument directly measures that standard, its status remains practiced only. The assignment demonstrates engagement; it does not constitute formal assessment.

Assessed is the highest tier a standard can reach within the coverage view. Reaching this tier means the standard has moved beyond practice into formal measurement — at least one quiz or assessment item is directly aligned to the standard, providing a mechanism by which student mastery can be evaluated and recorded. This is the target state for standards that are expected to be fully covered in a course.

Reaching assessed status is significant, but it does not necessarily mean coverage is complete in every sense. Even an assessed standard may carry an additional flag related to cognitive depth. If the assessment item aligned to a standard measures student performance at a lower Bloom's Taxonomy level than the standard itself requires — for instance, if the standard demands evaluation or synthesis but the quiz item only tests recall — the system may flag the standard for insufficient cognitive depth even though it technically holds assessed status. This means instructors should treat "assessed" as the necessary threshold for coverage, while also attending to whether the assessment item is challenging students at the right cognitive level. An assessed standard with a cognitive-depth flag is better than a practiced-only standard, but it still calls for improvement.

The most consequential boundary in the entire tiered system is the one separating practiced only from assessed, and it is essential to understand exactly where that boundary falls and why it exists. Practice through activities or assignments — regardless of how rigorous, relevant, or numerous those activities are — does not count as assessment. Only dedicated quiz or assessment items elevate a standard from practiced only to assessed. This is not an arbitrary technical rule; it reflects a meaningful pedagogical distinction. Practice gives students the opportunity to develop competency; assessment formally verifies whether that competency has been achieved. These are related but distinct functions, and the tiered system enforces that distinction by refusing to conflate them.

A standard that is practiced but not assessed describes a situation where students have engaged with the content but mastery has not been formally verified. This has real implications for reporting, accreditation, and instructional accountability. If a course is expected to produce evidence that students have mastered a given standard, practice activities alone cannot supply that evidence — only assessment data can. Moving a standard from practiced only to assessed therefore typically requires one concrete action: adding one or more quiz questions or assessment items that target the standard and do so at the cognitive level the standard demands. Once that item is in place and aligned, the standard's status updates to assessed, and the coverage gap is resolved.

  • Not aligned means no course connection exists at all — coverage work has not started.
  • Not addressed means the standard appears in course planning but has no instructional or assessment content linked to it — mapping is present, substance is not.
  • Practiced only means instructional activities or assignments engage with the standard — but no assessment item measures it, leaving mastery unverified.
  • Assessed means at least one quiz or assessment item formally measures the standard — this is the highest achievable status, though a cognitive-depth flag may still apply if the item's rigor falls short of what the standard requires.
  • The dividing line between practiced only and assessed is absolute: activities and assignments, no matter how thorough, cannot substitute for a dedicated assessment item when it comes to elevating a standard's tier.
