Understanding the Tiered Status System

▶

When tracking how well a course addresses its learning standards, a tiered status system provides a clear, structured way to understand exactly where each standard stands in the instructional design. Rather than relying on numerical scores or percentage-based completion bars, this system assigns every standard one of four precise labels — each label representing a meaningfully different relationship between the standard and the course materials aligned to it. Understanding what each tier means, and what causes a standard to move from one tier to another, is essential for making informed decisions about course design and curriculum coverage.

The four tiers, in order from least to most developed, are: not aligned, not addressed, practiced only, and assessed. A standard's position within this hierarchy is determined entirely by the type of course materials connected to it — not by how many materials exist, not by how much time is spent on the content, and not by any aggregate numeric measure. This distinction matters enormously in practice, because it means a single well-chosen alignment can advance a standard's status, while a large number of the wrong type of alignment will leave it stuck.

Not Aligned is the baseline state for any standard that has no connection whatsoever to the course. No assignments reference it, no quiz items measure it, and no activities engage students with it. This tier is essentially a flag that the standard has been entirely overlooked in the current course design. It does not necessarily mean the standard is irrelevant — it simply means no instructional materials have been associated with it at any level. When reviewing a course's standards coverage, any standard showing a not aligned status warrants attention to determine whether it should be incorporated or intentionally excluded.

Not Addressed represents a particularly important distinction in the system. A standard can be formally mapped to a course — meaning an instructor or curriculum designer has acknowledged its relevance and added it to the course's list of intended standards — and yet still sit at not addressed. Mapping alone does not move the needle. The standard must have at least one aligned piece of course material — an assignment, a quiz item, or an activity — before it can advance beyond this tier. Think of not addressed as the state of a standard that is on the agenda but has not yet made it into the actual instructional content. It highlights the gap between intention and execution: the standard is recognized, but students have not yet encountered it in any form within the course materials.

Practiced Only status means students are engaging with the standard through coursework, but that engagement is limited to activities. In this system, the word "practice" has a specific technical meaning — only activities qualify as practice. Assignments and quiz items are never classified as practice, regardless of their instructional purpose or difficulty level. A standard reaches practiced only when it has one or more aligned activities but no aligned assignments and no aligned quiz items. This tier signals a meaningful step forward from not addressed: students are actually working with the content. However, because there is no formal measurement mechanism attached to the standard — no graded assignment, no quiz — there is no way to determine whether students have actually mastered it. The course exposes students to the standard but stops short of holding them accountable for it. To move a standard out of practiced only, the course must include at least one aligned assignment or one aligned quiz item.

Assessed is the highest tier and indicates that the course not only engages students with the standard but also formally measures their understanding of it. A standard reaches assessed status as soon as it is aligned to any assignment or any quiz item — just one of either type is sufficient. There is no requirement to have both an assignment and a quiz item; one or the other is enough. If a standard already has an aligned assignment, it is already at assessed status, and adding a quiz item, while potentially valuable for other instructional reasons, is not required for the tier itself. Importantly, no matter how many activities are aligned to a standard, activities alone can never produce assessed status. Ten activities aligned to a standard still yields only practiced only, not assessed. The distinction between a quiz item and an activity, or between an assignment and an activity, is therefore the critical boundary in the entire tier system.

The logic governing tier transitions can be summarized clearly. The conditions are mutually exclusive and exhaustive — every standard will always fall into exactly one tier based on its current alignment state:

Alignment Condition Resulting Status Tier
No alignment of any kind (no assignments, quiz items, or activities) Not Aligned
Standard mapped to course, but no assignments, quiz items, or activities aligned Not Addressed
One or more activities aligned; no assignments and no quiz items aligned Practiced Only
At least one assignment or at least one quiz item aligned (with or without activities) Assessed

A crucial principle underlying the entire system is that type determines tier, not quantity. The system is deliberately precise in this regard. Adding more activities to a standard that is already at practiced only does not advance it to assessed — only changing the type of aligned material by introducing an assignment or quiz item accomplishes that. Similarly, a standard does not earn a higher tier by having more assignments or quiz items; even a single aligned assignment is enough to reach the maximum tier. This design keeps the status system focused on a meaningful pedagogical question: is this standard being measured in the course, or is it only being practiced?

To make these distinctions concrete, consider a few illustrative scenarios. Suppose a course covers a standard related to analyzing primary source documents. If the course includes a classroom discussion activity aligned to that standard but no graded essay or quiz, the standard sits at practiced only — students interact with the skill, but it is never formally evaluated. If the instructor then adds a short-answer quiz item aligned to the same standard, the status immediately advances to assessed. Alternatively, if the instructor adds a graded written assignment instead of a quiz item, the result is the same: the standard is now assessed. The path to assessed runs through assignments or quiz items — there is no shortcut through activities, no matter how rigorous or numerous those activities might be.

Another scenario worth considering: a new course is being built, and a standards framework has been attached to it with twenty standards listed. On day one, before any course materials are created, all twenty standards show as not addressed — they have been mapped to the course but have no material alignments yet. As the instructor builds out the course, some standards get connected to activities and immediately move to practiced only. Others get connected directly to assignments or quiz items and jump straight to assessed, potentially bypassing practiced only entirely. A standard does not need to pass through every tier sequentially; its status at any moment simply reflects whatever materials are currently aligned to it.

Understanding this tiered system empowers course designers and instructors to audit their curriculum with precision. By looking at the distribution of standards across all four tiers, it becomes easy to identify which standards are fully integrated into the course, which are only partially addressed, and which have been neglected entirely — and then to take targeted, deliberate action to close those gaps.

NotesThe tier system is purely qualitative — no numeric thresholds or weighted scoring exist. Emphasize throughout that the assignment/quiz item vs. activity distinction is the single most important boundary in the system, as it separates "practiced only" from "assessed." The "not addressed" vs. "not aligned" distinction is also frequently misunderstood and worth highlighting: course-level mapping without material alignment still leaves a standard at "not addressed," not "not aligned."
