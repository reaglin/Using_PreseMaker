Introduction to the Standards Coverage View

▶

When designing a course that must meet specific academic or institutional standards, one of the most persistent challenges instructors face is knowing, at any given moment, whether their materials are actually doing the job. It is easy to map standards to a course and assume coverage is adequate — but without a systematic way to audit that coverage, gaps can go unnoticed until students reach an assessment unprepared. The Standards Coverage View in PreseMaker is built specifically to address this problem. It functions as a dedicated audit interface, pulling together everything the platform knows about your mapped standards and your aligned course materials and presenting it in a single, organized view that makes gaps visible and actionable.

The Standards Coverage View is not simply a list of standards. It is a two-dimensional evaluation of your course's relationship to each standard it claims to address. The first dimension is alignment — has any course material (an assignment, a quiz item, or a graded activity) actually been connected to this standard? The second dimension is cognitive depth — even when material is aligned, does it engage students at the same level of intellectual complexity that the standard demands? A standard might call for students to evaluate competing arguments, but if every aligned item only asks students to recall definitions, the standard is technically covered but cognitively underpowered. The Standards Coverage View surfaces both of these concerns simultaneously, giving instructors a fuller picture of their course design than a simple checklist ever could.

Before diving into the specific components of the view, it is important to understand where the Standards Coverage View currently sits in PreseMaker's development roadmap. Like the Standards Library with which it works closely, the Standards Coverage View carries a 'System in Development' designation. This label is worth understanding correctly. It does not mean the feature is broken or experimental in a way that makes it unreliable for everyday use. The view is functional, and the information it surfaces is real and meaningful. What the designation signals is that the platform team continues to refine the feature — the presentation, the underlying calculations, or the available filtering options may evolve as the platform matures and as user feedback shapes priorities. Instructors who use the view today should feel confident in the data it presents while remaining open to the possibility that future updates will expand or adjust what they see. Treating the view as a living tool rather than a finished product is the right mindset.

The most immediately useful element of the Standards Coverage View is the headline summary that appears at the top of the interface. Rather than requiring an instructor to read through every row of every standard to form a picture of overall coverage, the headline aggregates all of the coverage data into a concise, course-level statement. It typically communicates two critical figures: the percentage of mapped standards that are assessed and the number of standards that have a cognitive-depth gap. For example, a headline might read something like: "62% of standards are assessed. 8 standards have a cognitive-depth gap." In just two numbers, an instructor learns that more than a third of their standards are not yet reaching the level of formal assessment, and that nearly a third of those that are assessed are still being addressed at the wrong cognitive level. This allows instructors to triage efficiently — knowing immediately that there is meaningful work to do without having to manually count rows or cross-reference multiple screens. The headline is a prioritization tool as much as it is a reporting tool.

Below the headline, the view presents each mapped standard as its own individual row. Every row is designed to give an instructor everything they need to understand that standard's coverage status without navigating away from the view. On the left, the row displays the standard's code — the alphanumeric identifier used by the relevant standards body — alongside the standard's full written statement. This pairing matters because codes alone are cryptic. Seeing both the code and the human-readable statement means an instructor can immediately recognize which skill or knowledge area is under discussion without needing to look it up separately.

To the right of the standard's identity information, each row also shows the cognitive (Bloom's) level assigned to the standard. Bloom's Taxonomy organizes intellectual tasks into a hierarchy — from lower-order thinking skills like remembering and understanding, up through applying and analyzing, to higher-order skills like evaluating and creating. By displaying the Bloom's level alongside alignment data, the view makes it possible to compare what the standard demands intellectually with what the aligned course materials actually require of students. Adjacent to the Bloom's level, the row displays counts of aligned assignments, quiz items, and activities — giving instructors a quick quantitative read on how much course material is touching each standard and what types of materials those are.

The most consequential element of each row is the Status field. This field condenses all of the alignment and activity data into a single, interpretive label that tells the instructor not just what is mapped but how well the standard is being served by current course materials. There are four status tiers, and understanding the distinction between them is essential for using the view effectively.

  • Not Aligned — This status means that nothing in the course has been mapped to this standard at all. The standard exists in the Standards Library and may be part of the course's declared framework, but no assignment, quiz item, or activity has been connected to it. From a course-design standpoint, this is the most urgent gap: the standard is entirely unaddressed.
  • Not Addressed — This status is subtly but importantly different from "Not Aligned." Here, the standard has been mapped to the course — it has been formally included in the course's standards framework — but no specific course material has yet been aligned to it. Think of it as a standard that has been declared but not yet delivered on. The instructor has acknowledged that this standard is relevant to the course, but students are not currently encountering it through any assignment, quiz, or activity.
  • Practiced Only — This status means that the standard has some alignment, but only through activities. In PreseMaker's framework, activities are typically lower-stakes, practice-oriented engagements — things students do to rehearse or explore a skill rather than to be formally evaluated on it. A "Practiced Only" status tells the instructor that students are encountering the standard in a formative context but that the standard has not yet been built into any graded assignment or quiz item. Students are practicing the skill but are never officially assessed on it.
  • Assessed — This is the fullest level of coverage. A standard reaches "Assessed" status when at least one assignment or quiz item — not merely an activity — is aligned to it. This means students are being formally evaluated on the standard, which is typically the threshold that institutions and accrediting bodies look for when verifying that a course genuinely covers its declared standards.

Together, these four status tiers create a graduated picture of coverage quality. A course in which most standards are "Assessed" but several remain "Practiced Only" or "Not Addressed" gives an instructor a clear and specific agenda: elevate the remaining standards by building them into graded assessments. A course in which many standards are "Not Aligned" points to a deeper design issue — the standards framework may have been selected but the alignment work has not yet begun in earnest.

It is also worth noting how the two-dimensional nature of the view — alignment plus cognitive depth — interacts with the Status field. A standard can be "Assessed" and still have a cognitive-depth gap. Imagine a standard designated at the Evaluate level of Bloom's Taxonomy. If the only aligned quiz items ask students to list facts or identify terms, those items are aligned in name but not in intellectual demand. The headline will flag this as a cognitive-depth gap even though the Status field shows "Assessed." This is precisely why the view presents both pieces of information: status tells you whether materials are aligned; the Bloom's level comparison tells you how well those materials match the standard's intent. Used together, these data points give instructors a genuinely actionable roadmap for refining course design rather than simply checking a compliance box.
