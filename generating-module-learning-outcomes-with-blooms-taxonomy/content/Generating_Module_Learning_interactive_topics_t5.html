Reviewing and Interpreting Generated Learning Outcomes

▶

When PreseMaker generates Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs) for a module, it does not produce arbitrary statements. Each outcome is the product of a structured generation logic that connects three elements: the source topic, a cognitive level drawn from Bloom's Taxonomy, and an action verb that makes the expected learning behavior observable and measurable. Reviewing these outcomes well requires more than a quick read-through. It demands a deliberate, criteria-driven evaluation that examines clarity, accuracy, Bloom's alignment, and overall scope. Instructors and instructional designers who develop this review skill become the critical quality gate between automated generation and a learning module that actually delivers on its educational promise.

Understanding what PreseMaker generates is the essential starting point for any meaningful review. The system ties each SLO to a specific module topic rather than to the module as a whole, which means every outcome has a traceable source. Alongside that topic association, PreseMaker assigns a Bloom's Taxonomy cognitive level — such as Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, or Create — and then selects an action verb appropriate to that level. The result is a statement like "Identify the three primary stages of the water cycle" (Remember/Understand level) or "Construct a data flow diagram that models a proposed system architecture" (Create level). Knowing this logic matters because it allows reviewers to distinguish between a deliberate design choice and a genuine error. For example, if a topic is introductory and PreseMaker assigns an Analyze-level outcome, that might reflect either an intentional push toward deeper thinking or an error in level assignment. Without understanding the generation logic, a reviewer might accept that misalignment without question or flag a correct choice incorrectly.

Checking clarity of generated outcomes is the first evaluative layer. A well-written SLO uses a specific, unambiguous action verb — one that points to a concrete, observable behavior. Verbs like list, compare, diagram, calculate, or defend describe behaviors that can be demonstrated and assessed. By contrast, verbs like know, understand, appreciate, or be familiar with describe internal mental states that cannot be directly observed or measured. When PreseMaker generates an outcome using one of these weak verbs, it is a clarity failure that must be corrected before the outcome can serve any meaningful instructional purpose.

Beyond verb choice, clarity depends on whether the outcome describes a single, discrete learning behavior. A statement like "Explain the causes of the French Revolution and evaluate the long-term political consequences for European governance" bundles two distinct cognitive tasks into one sentence. This is a compound outcome, and it creates problems at both the teaching and assessment stages: instructors cannot design a single assessment item that fairly captures both behaviors, and learners cannot target their study toward a focused expectation. Compound outcomes joined by and should always be separated into two independent SLOs.

Language accessibility is the third dimension of clarity. An outcome written for undergraduate nursing students should not be laden with advanced pharmacological terminology unless that terminology is itself a learning target. If a reviewer encounters an outcome whose phrasing would confuse the intended learner before they have even begun studying the topic, the language needs to be simplified. The outcome is a communication tool for the learner as much as it is a design tool for the instructor.

Verifying accuracy and topic alignment requires the reviewer to move back and forth between the generated SLO and the source topic. The core question is: does this outcome actually address the content described in the topic? Misalignment can take several forms. In a drift error, the outcome wanders into a related but distinct concept — for instance, a topic about network security protocols might produce an outcome focused on network hardware configuration, which belongs to a different topic entirely. In an omission error, the outcome captures only a peripheral aspect of the topic while ignoring its central concept. A topic on the stages of mitosis that generates only an outcome about the role of chromosomes has omitted the procedural, sequential knowledge that defines the topic.

Reviewers should also watch for representational errors, where the outcome subtly mischaracterizes the topic. If a topic covers a process that has known limitations or exceptions, but the generated outcome frames it as universally applicable, the SLO is not merely incomplete — it is inaccurate. These errors are particularly consequential because they will be carried forward into assessments and content that are built from the outcome.

Assessing the appropriateness of Bloom's level is often the most nuanced part of the review. Bloom's Taxonomy arranges cognitive skills along a spectrum of complexity: from recalling facts (Remember) through interpreting meaning (Understand), applying knowledge to new situations (Apply), breaking down components (Analyze), making judgments (Evaluate), and producing original work (Create). PreseMaker assigns one of these levels to each SLO, and the reviewer's job is to verify that the assignment makes sense given three factors: the verb used, the depth of content in the topic, and the learner's position in the broader curriculum.

Consider a module early in a course that introduces foundational vocabulary. An outcome at the Evaluate or Create level would demand cognitive sophistication that learners have not yet developed, making the outcome effectively unachievable. Conversely, an advanced module in a capstone course that generates only Remember-level outcomes is under-challenging learners and wasting the instructional opportunity. The Bloom's level should match not just the topic's depth but the learner's readiness.

A practical check is to test the verb against the assigned level. The table below shows representative action verbs for each Bloom's level to support this verification:

Bloom's Level Cognitive Focus Representative Action Verbs
Remember Recalling facts and basic concepts define, list, recall, recognize, state, identify
Understand Explaining ideas or concepts summarize, explain, paraphrase, classify, describe
Apply Using information in new situations solve, use, demonstrate, execute, implement, calculate
Analyze Drawing connections and breaking down components differentiate, compare, distinguish, examine, deconstruct
Evaluate Justifying decisions or positions critique, judge, defend, appraise, assess, justify
Create Producing new or original work design, construct, formulate, compose, develop, build

If an SLO uses the verb list but is labeled as an Analyze-level outcome, there is a mismatch. Either the verb needs to be upgraded (perhaps to differentiate or compare) or the Bloom's level assignment needs to be corrected. Both issues are actionable findings from the review.

Identifying common quality issues becomes faster with practice, but it helps to know the patterns to look for. Weak or unmeasurable verbs are the most frequent problem. Outcomes containing understand, know, appreciate, be aware of, or grasp the importance of must be revised. These verbs describe cognitive states rather than behavioral outputs and cannot be assessed reliably. For example, "Understand the importance of informed consent in clinical trials" should be revised to something like "Explain the legal and ethical requirements of informed consent in clinical trial design."

Compound outcomes are the second major quality issue. They are identifiable by the presence of coordinating conjunctions like and or as well as that link two distinct actions. "Identify the components of a business proposal and evaluate its feasibility for a given market" should become two separate outcomes: one at the Remember/Understand level addressing identification, and one at the Evaluate level addressing feasibility judgment. Separating them produces cleaner assessments and more honest learning expectations.

Scope creep is a subtler but equally problematic issue. An outcome that reads "Analyze all major theories of organizational behavior and their implications across historical, cultural, and economic contexts" is so broad that it could not be meaningfully addressed in a single module, let alone assessed in a single assignment. Overly broad outcomes signal that the generation process captured too much and that the reviewer needs to narrow the focus to what the topic genuinely covers.

Using outcomes as a quality benchmark for the module is the final and most integrative step in the review process. At this stage, the reviewer steps back from individual outcomes and evaluates the complete set as a coherent whole. The key questions are: Do the outcomes collectively cover the module's stated scope? Are there topics that have no corresponding outcome, creating a gap? Are there multiple outcomes that address essentially the same behavior, creating redundancy?

Beyond coverage, the reviewer should assess whether the outcomes, taken together, represent a coherent learning progression. A well-designed module typically moves learners through a logical sequence — building foundational knowledge before asking for application, establishing conceptual understanding before demanding analysis. If the full set of outcomes jumps erratically between cognitive levels without a discernible logic, the module's learning design may need restructuring, not just individual SLO edits.

The stakes of this review extend well beyond the outcomes document itself. In instructional design, outcomes function as the architectural blueprint from which assessments, instructional content, and learning activities are derived. An outcome that contains a weak verb will produce an assessment that measures nothing meaningful. An outcome that misrepresents a topic will cause instructors to develop content that teaches the wrong thing. An outcome set with significant gaps will leave learners underprepared for what follows the module in the broader course. Every quality issue identified and corrected during the SLO review stage prevents a cascade of downstream misalignments that become progressively more costly and difficult to fix the further into module development they are discovered.
